You found the Easter egg

A little story about the guy behind the code.

You already saw the résumé version. The neat bullets. The technologies. The percentages behaving themselves.

This is the messier version — how I went from moving data around, to understanding healthcare, to asking a slightly dangerous question: “Can we solve this with AI?”

No corporate buzzwords were harmed. A few were left unemployed.

01 / 2021 · THE START

It didn't start with AI. It started with data.

I joined TCS in 2021 as a Data Engineer. My first major project was Walgreens, and my playground was Hadoop — HDFS, MapReduce, Hive, Spark, Python and Shell.

At that point, I wasn't thinking about LLMs, agents or anything remotely resembling today's AI stack. I was thinking about pipelines, jobs, data quality and one very important question:

“Why is this taking two hours?”

One MapReduce workload eventually went from roughly 2 hours to 15 minutes. That was one of my first real lessons: sometimes the coolest engineering trick is simply making the thing stop wasting time.

2h → 15mtext processing

02 / 2021–23 · WALGREENS

I learned to make data behave.

Walgreens gave me the foundation that still follows me around today. I worked on large-scale text processing, ETL workflows and distributed systems, while automating repetitive work with Python, HiveQL and Shell.

There was a lot of Hadoop. There was a lot of debugging. And, occasionally, there was the timeless engineering experience of staring at a job and wondering what exactly it was doing with its life.

I also worked on data quality, consistency and reconciliation using Hive, Impala and Spark. That part mattered more than it sounds: if the data underneath the system is wrong, a very impressive application can still produce a very impressive wrong answer.

“First make the data trustworthy. Then make it clever.”
03 / 2023–25 · HEALTHCARE

Then healthcare made everything more interesting.

In 2023, I moved to the Kaiser Permanente account as a Data Engineer. This is where I stopped seeing healthcare as just another dataset.

I worked with 20TB+ of healthcare data, building and optimizing pipelines with Azure Data Factory, ADLS, Databricks, Delta Lake and PySpark. The work involved multiple source formats and enterprise systems — the sort of environment where “just load the data” is not really a complete architecture.

I also worked on Spark performance for 2M+ daily records, using techniques such as caching, partitioning and broadcast joins, while improving ETL efficiency and data validation.

04 / THE TURN

And then somebody said: “Learn AI.”

After spending years in Data Engineering, I started moving toward AI. At first, I did what pretty much every curious engineer does — I opened too many tabs.

LLMs. Generative AI. RAG. OCR. Document intelligence. Prompt engineering. Embeddings. Models. Frameworks. More models. More tabs.

But the interesting part wasn't learning the vocabulary. I wanted to understand what was actually happening underneath it — and whether I could build something useful with it.

“Okay... but can I actually build this?”

That question turned out to be much more important than any course title.

05 / THE GREEN TICKET

Then I got a ticket into the Innovation Lab.

Around the same time, a new innovation-focused cohort was taking shape within the TCS healthcare organization. I became part of it and eventually got my little green ticket into the TCS Healthcare Innovation Lab.

And suddenly the question changed.

“Where does AI actually make sense here?”

That distinction became important to me. A client problem doesn't arrive saying “Hello, I am an LLM use case.” It arrives as a workflow, a document, a backlog, a manual process or a very long email.

So the job became: understand the problem first, then decide whether the answer is an LLM, OCR, retrieval, document intelligence, automation — or sometimes not AI at all.

06 / AI, FOR REAL

I didn't just fall into AI. I kept digging.

The more I worked in the Innovation Lab, the more obvious it became that I genuinely liked this stuff. Not just the model. The whole machine around it.

I went deeper into LLM applications, RAG, embeddings, vector retrieval, OCR, document intelligence, AI agents, LangChain, LangGraph and Azure AI Foundry. Then came backend APIs, cloud services, queues, storage and the less glamorous bits that make a prototype survive outside a demo.

“An AI application is not a model with a prompt wearing a fancy shirt.”

My Data Engineering background kept showing up everywhere. Data pipelines became retrieval pipelines. Data quality became context quality. Distributed systems became part of AI architecture. The old stuff didn't disappear — it became useful in a different room.

07 / THE WORK NOW

Today, I live somewhere between the problem and the model.

Today I'm an AI SME / Team Lead – AI Engineer in the TCS Healthcare Innovation Lab, working directly with U.S. healthcare clients.

A big part of my work happens before anyone writes the first line of code: understand the client's problem, translate it into technical possibilities, design the architecture, prototype the idea, and then help the engineering team take it toward implementation and delivery.

The solutions I've worked on include Auto RFP, MR Extractor, handwritten prescription OCR, discharge bill processing, intelligent text processing, information extraction and workflow automation.

08 / THE BUILDER PART

Then there is the slightly unnecessary part.

I don't always stop building when work ends.

If something catches my attention, there is a decent chance I'll eventually think: “Hmm. I could build that.”

That's how I ended up publishing liuembeddings, a local embeddings and vector retrieval library built around Hugging Face Transformers and ChromaDB, and DbMeta, a zero-dependency Python engine for parsing and analyzing Hadoop, Spark and Databricks job metadata.

I also have projects around PySpark, SQL, Databricks, ETL, chatbots and analysis tools. Not every one of them needed a business case.

“This looks interesting. Let me build it.”
09 / THE HUMAN PART

Also: I'm not a walking architecture diagram.

A résumé is pretty bad at explaining this part.

I'm a fairly light-minded, easy-going person. I can get ridiculously serious when something technical refuses to work, but outside of that I'm not particularly interested in behaving like a corporate presentation that learned how to walk.

I like conversations, jokes, chess, gaming, random ideas and occasionally going down a completely unnecessary technical rabbit hole because one tiny thing looked interesting.

“Technology is the thing I'm serious about. My personality doesn't need to be.”
10 / THE SHORT VERSION

So... who am I?

On paper, I'm an AI SME / Team Lead. In the engineering trenches, I'm an AI Engineer. Underneath that, there's a pretty solid Data Engineering foundation. And somewhere in the middle is a person who happens to know quite a lot about healthcare data.

My path has basically been:

Data→Healthcare→Engineering→AI→Architecture→Leadership

And the funny thing is, I don't think the interesting part is finished yet.

There will probably be another weird problem, another rabbit hole, another prototype, another “can we build this?”

I'll probably say yes before thinking about how much work that is.
