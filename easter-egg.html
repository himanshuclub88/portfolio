The portfolio gives you the clean version: Data Engineering, healthcare, AI, architecture, projects, technologies.

Less “professional summary”. More “how did this guy end up here?”

01 / THE BORDER
Before the engineer, there was a lot of moving around.

I grew up between places.

I was born in Nepal, near the border, and spent my early school years in Biratnagar. My family roots are in Uttar Pradesh, though, so “where are you from?” has never been a particularly simple question for me.

For school, my life crossed that border quite literally. From nursery through eighth, I studied in Biratnagar. For ninth and tenth, I was travelling into India by school bus — roughly a long commute that could take more than an hour and, depending on traffic, sometimes much longer.

After tenth, I moved again for eleventh and twelfth, staying with my cousin Dara Singh, who was working with the Indian Railways. Then came Greater Noida for B.Tech, followed by the different chapters of my TCS journey: Delhi, Varanasi and eventually Hyderabad.

Nepal→ UP roots→ Greater Noida→ Delhi→ Varanasi→ Hyderabad
“I moved a lot. So adapting became normal.”
02 / THE ACCIDENTAL COMPUTER SCIENTIST
Class 11 · one decision that changed everything.

I didn't have some grand plan to become a software engineer.

In class 11, I had to choose between Hindi and Computer Science. I had studied Sanskrit in tenth, and I wasn't comfortable choosing Hindi because I thought I might struggle with it. My school didn't offer Sanskrit as the option I wanted.

So I chose Computer Science.

And then I met Python.

That turned out to be a very fortunate accident. I liked it enough to choose B.Tech Computer Science, got into Noida Institute of Engineering and Technology through the Uttar Pradesh engineering entrance route, and started building the foundation that eventually became my career.

“I chose Computer Science for a practical reason. Then I discovered I actually liked it.”
03 / THE ENGINEER
2021 · TCS · Data Engineering

Then the accidental choice became a profession.

I joined TCS in 2021 as a Data Engineer. My first major project was Walgreens, where the world was Hadoop, HDFS, MapReduce, Hive, Spark, Python and Shell.

This was not the glamorous version of engineering. It was jobs, pipelines, text processing, automation, data quality, reconciliation and figuring out why something that should have finished already was still running.

One of my early wins was taking a text-processing workload from roughly two hours to fifteen minutes. That experience stuck with me. Good engineering is often less about making something look impressive and more about making something stop wasting time.

2h → 15mtext processing improvement

04 / HEALTHCARE
2023–25 · Kaiser Permanente

Then the scale got serious.

In 2023, I moved into the Kaiser Permanente healthcare project. Suddenly the data wasn't just large. It was healthcare data, with the reliability, quality and operational expectations that come with it.

I worked with 20TB+ of healthcare data, building and optimizing ingestion and transformation pipelines with Azure Data Factory, ADLS, Databricks, Delta Lake and PySpark.

Daily processing crossed 2 million records. I worked on performance, partitioning, caching, broadcast joins, validation and the practical reality of moving enterprise data through distributed systems.

Looking back, this period gave me something that became unexpectedly useful later: I learned to think about data not as a file or table, but as the foundation everything else depends on.

20TB+healthcare data
2M+records processed daily

05 / THE TURN TO AI
The question changed.

Then somebody told me to learn AI.

After years of Data Engineering, I started moving toward AI. At first it was the usual flood: LLMs, Generative AI, RAG, OCR, embeddings, document intelligence, prompt engineering, models, frameworks and a browser full of tabs.

But I wasn't satisfied with knowing the vocabulary. I wanted to know what was actually happening underneath it and, more importantly, whether I could build something useful with it.

My data background made the transition feel natural in some ways. Retrieval looked like another kind of data pipeline. Context quality started looking a lot like data quality. AI applications still needed storage, APIs, orchestration and all the engineering underneath the model.

“Okay... but can I actually build this?”
06 / THE GREEN TICKET
TCS Healthcare Innovation Lab

Then I got a ticket into the Innovation Lab.

I became part of an innovation-focused cohort within the TCS healthcare organization and eventually got my little green ticket into the TCS Healthcare Innovation Lab.

That changed the question again.

“Where does AI actually make sense here?”

A real client problem doesn't arrive labelled “LLM use case”. It arrives as a manual workflow, a document, a backlog, a long email, a repetitive process or a problem someone has been tolerating for years.

So my job became much broader than choosing a model. Understand the problem. Design the solution. Decide whether the answer is an LLM, OCR, retrieval, document intelligence, automation — or sometimes not AI at all.

07 / THE WORK NOW
AI SME / Team Lead – AI Engineer

Today, I live somewhere between the problem and the model.

Today I'm working as an AI SME / Team Lead – AI Engineer in the TCS Healthcare Innovation Lab, working with U.S. healthcare clients.

A large part of the work happens before the first model call: understanding requirements, translating business problems into technical possibilities, designing architecture, prototyping, guiding implementation and helping move useful ideas toward production.

I've worked on solutions including Auto RFP, MR Extractor, handwritten prescription OCR, discharge bill processing, intelligent text processing, information extraction and workflow automation.

08 / THE OTHER RABBIT HOLE
2015 → now · completely unrelated to the résumé

There is another subject I follow almost as seriously as technology.

Defense.

I started getting seriously interested around 2015. Since then, I've followed developments in aircraft, tanks, missiles, submarines, drones, radar, air-to-air systems, air-to-surface systems, surface-to-air systems, anti-tank systems, ballistic missiles, quasi-ballistic missiles, hypersonic systems and modern warfare.

The interesting part isn't simply knowing the names. I like understanding why a technology exists, what advantage it gives, what changed from the previous generation, and how different systems interact.

A typical Himanshu thought process

New capability appears → understand what it changes → understand why it matters → think about the counter → go back and learn the history → somehow end up watching another hour of defense analysis.

“What is the advantage of this, and what counters it?”

I follow defense communities where conversations can get technical enough that a casual viewer might understand very little of it. I enjoy that level of detail. Even something as specific as the difference between gallium arsenide and gallium nitride can turn into a surprisingly long conversation.

09 / THE LEARNER
Not everything has to be useful.

Sometimes I learn things simply because I can.

I've learned things late that people usually associate with childhood: how to solve a Rubik's Cube, how an abacus works, and even a programming language like Prolog just because I wanted to understand it.

Chess came into my life around 2021. I'm still learning. I'm not a beginner anymore, but I'm definitely not walking around calling myself a grandmaster either.

I also learned about farming after spending time in my village in Uttar Pradesh. My father worked in Biratnagar, while my family and village life remained tied to UP. So I've had exposure to a world that is very different from data pipelines and AI applications.

Not every thing I learn needs to become a project, a certification or a line on LinkedIn. Sometimes the reason is simply:

“I don't know how this works. I want to know.”

10 / THE ADAPTABLE GUY
The part that probably doesn't belong on LinkedIn.

I can fit into a lot of places.

I've lived in different cities, worked from different setups, travelled between different environments and spent enough of my life moving around that adaptation became normal.

I'm open to different cultures. I like people. I can stay at home and be perfectly happy, or I can go out and roam around. I like talking to people, and I don't need everything around me to be familiar before I feel comfortable.

Food is probably the easiest example. I like almost everything. Put me somewhere new, give me the local food, and I'm generally going to try it rather than start looking for the nearest version of what I already know.

“Different place? Different people? Different food? Fine. I'll adjust.”

Maybe that comes from moving around so much. Maybe it's just how I'm wired. Either way, adaptability is one of the things I value most about myself.

11 / THE PROBLEM SOLVER
The thing the résumé still can't quite describe.

If I had to describe myself without using my job title, I'd start here.

I'm a problem solver.

Not because every problem magically becomes easy, and not because I've solved everything. I just have a lot of confidence that if something is difficult, unfamiliar or messy, I can sit with it long enough to figure it out.

That trait shows up in different places: engineering, AI architecture, defense research, chess, learning a new tool, understanding a new system, or simply adapting to a completely different environment.

If someone spends a whole day with me, I hope they notice three things:

Problem solving I naturally try to understand how to get from “this is a problem” to “here is a way through it.”
Adaptability New place, new people, new situation — I don't need everything to be familiar.
Curiosity When something genuinely interests me, I want to understand it properly.
“My résumé tells you what I work with. It doesn't tell you how I approach the world.”
12 / THE SHORT VERSION

So... who is the guy behind the code?

He's someone who was born in Nepal, has family roots in Uttar Pradesh, crossed borders for school, moved cities for education and work, and somehow ended up building a career around something he first discovered almost by accident in class 11.

He's a Data Engineer by foundation, an AI Engineer by the work he does now, and an AI SME / Team Lead by responsibility.

Outside work, he follows defense development with an enthusiasm that can get surprisingly technical, plays chess, likes learning strange things, knows a bit about farming, likes roaming around, likes staying home, likes talking to people, and will happily eat whatever food is put in front of him.

More than any particular technology, though, there are three things that probably explain him best:

Problem Solver· Adaptable· Curious

The career path looks neat when you write it backwards:

Data→Healthcare→ Engineering→AI→ Architecture→Leadership

The actual path was less neat.

It involved moving countries and cities, choosing Computer Science for a surprisingly practical reason, discovering Python, learning data engineering, being told to learn AI, finding the Innovation Lab, and then spending a completely unreasonable amount of free time learning about things that have absolutely nothing to do with healthcare software.

And I don't think that part is finished.

There will probably be another problem worth solving, another system worth understanding, another defense development worth following, another chess position worth figuring out, or another completely unnecessary thing that makes me think:

“Hmm. Let me learn that.”
