You found the Easter egg

A little story about the guy behind the code.

You already saw the résumé version. This isn't that. This is the slightly more human version — how a Data Engineer ended up becoming an AI SME, why healthcare became part of the story, and why I still like building things just because I can.

No corporate buzzwords were harmed while making this page.

01 / BEFORE AI

It didn't start with AI.

My professional journey started in 2021 at TCS, where I began as a Data Engineer. My first major project was with Walgreens, working with large-scale data in the Hadoop ecosystem.

MapReduce, Hive, Spark, Python, Shell — that was my world. I wasn't sitting there thinking, “One day I'll become an AI SME.” I was busy solving data problems, optimizing jobs, automating workflows, and figuring out why something that should take minutes was taking hours.

That phase taught me something I still use every day: think like an engineer before you think about the technology.

“Okay, but why is this taking two hours?”

At Walgreens, I worked on large-scale text processing and data pipelines, including a MapReduce workload that was brought down from roughly two hours to about fifteen minutes.

02 / HEALTHCARE

Then healthcare became part of the story.

In 2023, I moved to the Kaiser Permanente account as a Data Engineer. This was where my relationship with healthcare data became much deeper.

I worked with large-scale healthcare datasets and enterprise data platforms using Azure Data Factory, ADLS, Databricks, Delta Lake, Spark and PySpark. I was working with the kind of infrastructure where data isn't just data — it has to be processed reliably, consistently and at scale.

More importantly, I started understanding the domain. Healthcare workflows, healthcare data, the messiness of real-world information, and the challenges that appear when you move from a clean demo to an enterprise environment.

“I didn't know it then, but this domain knowledge was going to become one of my biggest advantages in AI.”
03 / THE TURN

Then someone asked me to learn AI.

After spending years in Data Engineering, my manager approached me about learning AI.

And that's where things started moving in a different direction.

I started exploring LLMs, Generative AI, RAG, OCR, document intelligence, prompt engineering and AI applications. But I wasn't really interested in just collecting course certificates or memorizing terminology.

I wanted to understand what was actually happening — and, more importantly, I wanted to build things.

“Okay... but can I actually build this?”
04 / THE GREEN TICKET

The Innovation Lab chapter.

Around the same time, within the TCS healthcare organization, a new initiative was taking shape. A team and cohort were being created around innovation and emerging technology.

I became part of that cohort, and eventually got the opportunity — my little green ticket — to work with the TCS Healthcare Innovation Lab.

That changed the kind of problems I was exposed to.

I was no longer learning AI only as a technology. I was seeing real business problems from healthcare clients and asking a much more interesting question:

“Where does AI actually make sense here?”

Sometimes the answer was an LLM. Sometimes OCR. Sometimes document intelligence. Sometimes retrieval. Sometimes a workflow around the model. And sometimes the answer was simply: don't use AI here.

05 / AI, FOR REAL

And then I realized I was kind of a hardcore AI guy.

The more I worked in the Innovation Lab, the more I realized that I genuinely enjoyed this stuff.

Not just the models. The whole system around them.

I started going deeper into LLM applications, RAG architectures, embeddings, vector retrieval, OCR, document intelligence, agents, LangChain, LangGraph, Azure AI Foundry, local models, backend APIs, cloud services and the engineering required to make all of it useful.

And my old Data Engineering background kept showing up in unexpected places.

“An AI application isn't just a model with a prompt.”

Data, retrieval, APIs, queues, storage, security, integration, scalability, monitoring and workflow all matter. The model is one piece of the puzzle.

06 / WHAT I DO NOW

Today, I work somewhere between the problem and the model.

I'm currently working as an AI SME / Team Lead – AI Engineer within the TCS Healthcare Innovation Lab, working with U.S. healthcare clients.

My role is broader than simply writing AI code. I work with clients to understand what they're actually trying to solve, translate that into technical possibilities, think through architecture, explore the right AI approach, prototype solutions, and lead the team toward implementation and delivery.

07 / THE BUILDER PART

I don't always stop building when work ends.

One thing about me: if I find a technology interesting, I tend to experiment with it outside of work too.

I've built and published open-source Python projects such as Liu Embeddings, focused on local embeddings and vector retrieval, and DbMeta, a zero-dependency engine for parsing and analyzing Hadoop, Spark and Databricks job metadata.

I also have smaller projects around PySpark, SQL, Databricks, ETL, chatbots and analysis tools.

Not every project needs a business case. Sometimes the business case is simply: “This looks interesting. Let me build it.”

08 / THE HUMAN PART

And no, I'm not serious all the time.

This is probably the part a résumé is terrible at explaining.

I'm actually a pretty light-minded and easy-going person. I can get extremely serious when I'm solving a technical problem, but outside of that, I'm not really interested in acting like a walking corporate presentation.

I like conversations, jokes, random ideas, chess, gaming, and occasionally going down a completely unnecessary technical rabbit hole just because something caught my attention.

Technology is the thing I'm serious about. My personality doesn't need to be.

09 / THE SHORT VERSION

So, who am I?

If you only look at my title, I'm an AI SME / Team Lead. If you look at my background, I'm a Data Engineer. If you look at my current work, I'm an AI Engineer. If you look at my projects, I'm a guy who likes building things. If you look at my healthcare experience, I'm someone who understands the domain behind the technology.

Honestly, all of those are true.

My journey has basically gone from data → healthcare → engineering → AI → architecture → leadership.

And I'm still figuring out where it goes next.

That's probably the fun part.
